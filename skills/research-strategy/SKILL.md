# Choose a question that can change your mind

Name the current bottleneck precisely. “Need a clever argument” is not actionable; “must show every extremizer admits this decomposition” is. Separate the final objective from the smallest missing statement that would advance it.

Keep a few live explanations or routes. For each, identify the prerequisite, the cheapest discriminating test, and what result would make you abandon or revise it. Prefer an action that separates plausible explanations over another sample that all explanations already predict.

Exploit structure before increasing brute force: invariants, symmetry, scaling, extremizers, dual formulations, prime-power components, monotonicity, or a smaller equivalent statement. These are questions to ask, not a checklist every problem must satisfy.

Search literature when a bottleneck appears to be a standard structural result, when the needed mechanism is unfamiliar, or before claiming a new classification. First formulate the precise statement you need so that you can judge whether a source actually supplies it.

After a failed route, diagnose the failure. A false lemma, a missing estimate, a bad implementation, and an unavailable provider require different responses. A local defect may deserve repair within the same route. Record a genuinely obstructed route with its assumptions and reopen condition; changing the route's name is not new mathematics.

Continue if there is a concrete, evidence-based next test. Seek an independent agent when it can attack a different route or audit a specific weak step. Pause with a useful obstruction when available routes have no defensible next step or resources are exhausted. Never infer impossibility from your own lack of progress.
